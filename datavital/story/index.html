The problem

Hospitals lose money and beds every day, and find out a month later.

Data sits in the patient record, the lab, radiology, pharmacy, billing and finance, each in its own system. Reports arrive weeks late and nobody can see the whole patient journey, so the same problems repeat.

0%of insurance claims can be rejected on first submission. Cash is locked up and costs more to collect.
0%of emergency patients stay 4 hours or more. Crowding, wasted capacity and unhappy patients.
0hospitals and 80,072 beds in the Kingdom run on reports that are late and systems that don't talk to each other.

Sources: KPMG (2026); a study of a Saudi government hospital emergency department, PMC10947632 (2024); General Authority for Statistics (2023).

The missing piece is not more data. It is the step from a number to a decision: what is wrong, where, why, what to do, who does it, and did it work.

How it works

From a number to a named owner, in six steps.

This is one real run on the demo hospital, DataVital Riyadh 1. Nobody wrote it: the agents, the investigator and the brain found it from the hospital's own records.

1

What is wrong?

Each area's agent watches its standards after every data release and flags what moved the wrong way.

ExampleEmergency: seen by a doctor within CTAS time fell from 67.6% to 62.0% in 30 days.
2

Where exactly?

The investigator splits the change by hour, ward, payer, test, scanner type or theatre, so the problem has an address.

ExampleMost of the fall is in patients arriving 11:00–19:00: 53% were seen in time, down from 65%.
3

Why?

It checks the usual causes over the same weeks and says which did not change, and which did.

ExampleMore patients, sicker patients, waits for ward beds and staff hours did not explain it. Beds in use rose from 71.0% to 74.8%.
4

Where does it start?

The brain links findings across areas along the patient's way through the hospital, and points to the first step.

ExampleStays longer than expected on General Surgery and Internal Medicine (31.3% → 37.3%), so more beds in use, longer waits for a ward bed, admitted patients waiting in the emergency department, fewer seen in time.
5

What to do, and who?

One action at the start of the chain, with the owner by role. One click puts it on the hospital's action plan.

ExampleReview every stay past its expected length at the daily round and name what it waits for. Owner: Director of Nursing.
6

Did it work?

The agents check the whole chain again in 30 days, and the leadership brief shows how the area has done since the decision.

ExampleIf it works, the rest of the chain follows in order: beds in use, then the wait for a ward bed, then the emergency department.

It shows a likely chain, not proof. People decide; nothing is changed without a person. Demo data for 22 synthetic hospitals.

The AI

Each piece of AI sits on the screen where the work happens, says why, suggests the next step, and is checked against what really happened. People decide.

Command centre · Area agents and the brain

Seven agents watch their areas. The brain finds the problems that are really one.

  • One agent each for emergency, wards and beds, intensive care, surgery, laboratory, radiology and the revenue cycle
  • Each says Needs attention, Watching or Steady, with the finding: the standard, before → now, and where most of the change is
  • The brain links wrong-way findings along the patient's way through the hospital into one chain, and marks where it starts
  • One action at the start of the chain, the owner, and Add to action plan; findings that do not link are listed on their own
  • Ask Vital Assist explains the chain from what the agents found, judging each figure only against its own target

Leadership brief · Why it changed

Press Why it changed on any priority and the investigator does the first hour of analysis.

  • Splits the change by the part that carries it: arrival hour, ward, payer, test, scanner type or theatre
  • Checks the usual causes over the same weeks: more patients, sicker patients, bed waits, staff hours, analyzer or scanner stops
  • Says plainly what the data cannot show yet, so nobody draws the wrong conclusion
  • One suggested action with its owner, added to the action plan in one click and checked again in 30 days
  • Covers 25 standards; the opening paragraph is built from the figures only, not written by a model

Wards, now · Who is likely to go home today

Every patient on the wards gets a chance of going home before midnight.

  • The chance comes from how often stays like this one ended that day before: ward, day of stay, age, operation, ICU, ready, order, open consults
  • Each patient has the reason and the next step: write the discharge order, or ask the team for a decision on the round
  • Likely today, likely tomorrow and likely but held up, next to what the old rule of thumb says
  • Refreshed every 5 minutes; on the Bed outlook screen, Was the going-home prediction right? compares each morning's chance with who actually went home

Claims and denials · Before the claim is sent

Claims likely to be denied are caught while they can still be fixed.

  • Every billed claim not yet sent gets a chance of denial, the reason and the fix: attach the discharge summary, send before the payer's 60-day limit
  • Most money at risk first: chance × value
  • Was the warning right? On the demo data, checking the riskiest 5% of claims would catch 53% of denials; of every 100 claims warned about, 54 were denied
  • Denials warned about, by reason, so the team sees which ones the check can and cannot catch

Claims and denials · Appeal drafts

Every denied claim gets a draft appeal letter, written from the bill and the insurer's answer.

  • The facts of the denial: amount claimed, charges before discount, days left to appeal, and how often appeals for this reason were won
  • The letter, with every figure checked against the bill; boxes in yellow are for the team to fill in
  • What to attach for this reason, then Copy letter, Write it again or Mark as sent, which records it on the action plan
  • Nothing is sent from DataVital: a person reviews the letter and sends it through the payer's portal
